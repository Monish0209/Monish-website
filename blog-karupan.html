Karuppan And His Raayappan

By Monish • 13 Min Read

“Oru naalutha ponnu, aaru maasathu ponnu, pathu varushathu ponnu — moonu peru onna, ore veetla vayasukku varanga. Adhu enna?”

A one-year-old girl, a six-month-old girl, and a ten-year-old girl — all three of them attain puberty together in the same house. What is it?

Now, tell me honestly—wouldn't that leave you completely baffled?

Well, this was the riddle Mr. Raayappan threw at us while we were trekking through the forests of Jawadhu Hills, Tamil Nadu.

We had come to Jawadhu Hills with a rather different mission: to photograph the Milky Way. We had chosen a tent-stay experience in the hills, where we could spend the night under the open sky and enjoy a quiet getaway in the middle of nature.

But the sky had other plans.

Almost as soon as we arrived, heavy thunderstorms rolled in. For the next forty-eight hours, the clouds refused to clear, leaving us with practically no chance of seeing the Milky Way, let alone photographing it. So, we simply went ahead with the other activities that came with our stay.

And that was how we came to know Mr. Raayappan.

He was the old caretaker who welcomed us when we arrived, helped set up our tent, and even prepared our dinner that night. The following morning, however, he took on a different role. As we set out into the forest for our trek, Raayappan became our guide.

And he wasn't alone.

His loyal dog, Karuppan, had been with us throughout our stay. But out in the forest, we saw a completely different side of him. Full of energy, he raced ahead of us, leading the way as though every trail belonged to him.

What followed was a trek filled with riddles, songs, stories, knowledge, laughter, and countless little moments that made us forget all about the Milky Way we had come looking for.

This is the story of two souls we met in the Jawadhu Hills—Raayappan and his Karuppan—and the forest they seemed to own.

On 1st September, we planned to visit Jawadhu Hills because of its proximity to the Vainu Bappu Observatory, which is operated by the Indian Institute of Astrophysics. The observatory is one of the important centres for astronomical research in India, and the area around it has Bortle Class 4 skies. That makes it reasonably good for viewing and photographing the Milky Way under favourable conditions.

Hoping for that, we booked a unique stay at Gogo Camping, which was located quite close to the observatory. It was a tent stay, which meant we could spend the whole night under the open sky, photographing the stars, talking, and simply enjoying the night. For us, it sounded like a perfect weekend getaway.

On cue, we started our journey from Coimbatore in my beloved Garuda—my Citroën C3—and reached Jawadhu Hills by afternoon. We were first welcomed with lunch at Moon Garden Restaurant in Vellore, which was also owned by the same campsite. After lunch, we headed to the Vainu Bappu Observatory. There were a few other tourists with us, and we joined a tour of the observatory.

We also attended a small seminar about how the telescopes work and what actually happens at the observatory. Thanks to all the preparation and reading I had already done for Milky Way photography, I found myself answering almost every question thrown at us by the person conducting the seminar.

It was a nice little moment, especially because I had come there with a genuine interest in understanding the night sky, not just photographing it.

By the time we finished the visit, it was already getting late. We didn't have enough time to explore any other place and still make it back to the campsite before dark. So we decided to head back and spend the evening at the campsite.

The ride to the campsite was not exactly a smooth one. There were uneven roads, rough off-road stretches, loose soil, and quite a few places where the road simply didn't feel like a road anymore.

But, as usual, Garuda kept up its reputation. With 180 mm of ground clearance and the suspension doing its job beautifully, the C3 glided through the rough patches without much trouble, and we finally entered the campsite.

The moment the gate opened, someone came sprinting towards us.

It was Karuppan—a black country dog who was apparently the security guard of the campsite.

To be precise, I was petrified.

I'm not exactly a great fan of dogs because of a few incidents from my past. So seeing this black dog running straight towards me wasn't exactly the welcome I was hoping for.

And that was also when I met Raayappan.

He was the old caretaker of the campsite, and as we soon learned, Karuppan was his dog. While Karuppan was running all over the campsite, Raayappan was busy doing practically everything else. He started setting up our tent, preparing things for the evening, and taking care of the campsite as if there was nothing else in the world to worry about.

The four of us—my brother, my friend and cousin Surjit, my uncle Kalai, and me—settled into the place and started to relax in a small hut nearby.

By then, the sky was still clear.

And for me, that meant only one thing.

I started calculating.

The direction. The angle. The position of the Milky Way. Where I could place the camera. How I could frame the shot.

Everything was falling into place.

At least, that's what I thought.

Slowly, the sky started changing.

The clouds began gathering, and the blue above us became darker and darker. We had also been tired after the long drive, so once our tent was ready and the weather still seemed manageable, we decided to get some sleep.

We slept for almost two hours.

And then we woke up to the sound of rain hitting the tent.

There was something wonderfully romantic about it. Lying inside a tent while rain poured outside and the drops kept hitting the fabric above us was, in its own way, a beautiful experience.

But there were two problems.

One, I wasn't with my wife. I was with my uncle.

And two, I had come all the way to Jawadhu Hills to photograph the Milky Way.

That rain meant my mission was probably over.

Cursing my fate, I stepped out and went back to the little hut, where Raayappan was sitting. We spent some time talking, while he prepared tea for us and served it with biscuits.

And somewhere between that cup of tea, the rain outside, and Raayappan's stories, the night started taking a very different direction from what I had planned.

After the tea and biscuits, we rested for a while. Raayappan had a big speaker at the campsite, so we connected to it, played some songs, and started enjoying ourselves. For a while, we completely forgot about the rain and the Milky Way.

But as the night fell, the rain showed no intention of stopping.

By then, I was slowly coming to terms with the fact that I probably wasn't going to take any photographs that night. Until then, some part of me was still hoping that the clouds would suddenly clear and I would somehow get the Milky Way shot I had come for. But with every passing hour, that hope was disappearing little by little.

The campsite was unusually quiet that evening. It was the off-season, and the weather had probably scared away most of the visitors. Usually, the place could have close to a hundred tents, but that night there were only two tents occupied when we arrived.

Later in the evening, we noticed Raayappan setting up two more tents. So, clearly, there were still a few more people on their way.

Dinner was a small buffet arranged by Raayappan and the other people at the campsite. Even the campfire, which would normally have been part of the evening, was out of the question because of the rain.

While we were having dinner, another group arrived—four men of different ages, almost like our own group. There was a little commotion between them and the campsite staff about the food and some other things. We decided it was none of our business and quietly moved away.

We went back to the little hut instead of our tents.

We played some card games, joked around, and eventually got into one of those unexpected deep conversations that happen when a group of friends sits together late at night. We talked about life, work, money, relationships, and pretty much everything else.

Before we knew it, almost two hours had passed.

It was already midnight when we finally called it a night and went back to our respective tents to sleep.

Outside, the rain was still falling.

Morning came, and honestly, I don't know what woke me up. I just opened my eyes and noticed that everyone else was still sleeping.

So I carefully got out without disturbing anyone. I picked up my camera and started wandering around the campsite. I wasn't looking for anything in particular. I wasn't searching for a photograph either. I just wanted to have my camera with me, so that if something happened, I could record it and document it.

And then Karuppan appeared again.

He came running towards me, but this time he didn't scare me. He simply joined me on my walk and started wandering around the campsite with me.

A little later, Raayappan told us that we should go for a morning trek.

I went back to the tents and found that my brother was already awake. We woke everyone else up, got ready, and soon all of us were set for the trek.

It wasn't really a proper trek into some deep, dense forest. It was more like a regular forest trail around the campsite, one that the locals used to walk through regularly. And this time, we started walking with two guides—Raayappan and Karuppan.

The moment we entered the forest, Raayappan seemed like a different person.

The previous evening, he had been busy with everything at the campsite—setting up tents, preparing food, taking care of the guests, and doing whatever work needed to be done. So we hadn't really had the chance to talk to him much.

But now, away from all that work, his mood became much lighter.

And that mood slowly transferred to us too.

We started talking freely with him, and gradually, we also got acquainted with the other group that had arrived the previous night. The little commotion we had seen between them and the campsite staff suddenly seemed irrelevant. They were actually a good bunch of people.

One of them was from the film industry and had worked in movies like Viduthalai. Another was a manager at a Kia car showroom. There was also a pawnshop owner among them. One of them didn't join us for the trek, but the rest of us slowly started talking and getting to know each other.

And somehow, that was also because of Raayappan.

He had created that mood.

We walked down from the southern side of the campsite, made a big loop through the forest, and eventually entered the campsite again from the northern side. The entire trek took around two and a half hours, including the stops we made along the way. We started at around 7 in the morning and returned around 9:30.

As we walked, Raayappan started telling us stories about the campsite and the people who had visited before us.

Just a week earlier, he told us, there had been hundreds of people at the campsite. Only a handful of them had gone trekking with him, but there had been a huge campfire at night. People were dancing, drinking, and smoking.

He especially complained about some of the young girls who had come there, drinking and smoking. He couldn't really digest it. Coming from the kind of life he had lived and the generation he belonged to, he found the changes in today's culture difficult to understand.

We kept talking as we walked.

At one point, we were making a fairly steep climb. We were already putting in some effort to get up the slope when Raayappan suddenly asked us a riddle.

“Oru naalutha ponnu, aaru maasathu ponnu, pathu varushathu ponnu — moonu peru onna, ore veetla vayasukku varanga. Adhu enna?”

A one-day-old girl, a six-month-old girl, and a ten-year-old girl — all three of them attain puberty together in the same house. What is it?

We were completely baffled.

We kept thinking about it again and again, trying to figure out what on earth he was talking about.

Luckily, I have always loved riddles. I was introduced to them long before I even knew what a riddle was. I grew up spending time with my grandparents, my great-grandparents on my mother's side, and many other older people in my mother's native village. Riddles were a part of those conversations.

So I started thinking about the words he had used.

When he said that all three of them were "attaining puberty," I felt there had to be some connection to a change in colour or appearance.

And suddenly, it clicked.

The one-day-old girl was edible lime, the lime paste that is used with betel leaves.

The six-month-old girl was the betel leaf, which takes around six months to grow.

And the ten-year-old girl was the betel nut.

When these three are put together and chewed, they turn the mouth red.

That was what Raayappan meant by "attaining puberty."

It was an old tradition that people of his generation were familiar with. After a heavy meal or simply as part of the habit, they would chew betel leaf, betel nut, and edible lime together.

I looked at him and gave him my answer.

He smiled and said, “Seriya sonna, kannu.” — You got it right, my boy.

And by then, we had already finished climbing the slope and continued walking through the forest.

As we continued our trek, we kept seeing Karuppan everywhere.

Sometimes he would race ahead of us as if he had somewhere very important to go. At other times, he would suddenly leave the trail, walk sideways into the forest, disappear for a while, and then somehow join us again.

If we were the main river flowing along the trail, Karuppan was like a small branch of that river—detaching itself from the main flow, wandering somewhere else, and joining us again a little later.

He was an incredibly active dog.

And sometimes, his energy even got him into trouble.

Raayappan would scold him in Tamil: "ஏ, அப்படி போ. சும்மா காலுக்குள்ள காலுக்குள்ள வந்து பூந்திராதே!" — "Hey, go that way. Don't keep coming between their legs and tripping someone!"

It was almost like Raayappan was having a conversation with him.

And strangely enough, Karuppan seemed to understand.

After being scolded, he would move away from us and continue doing his own thing. A few minutes later, he would come back near us again, but this time he would make sure not to get in our way.

Karuppan was having the time of his life.

At one point during the trek, we reached a turning where there was a small uphill trail. Raayappan told us that the main route to the campsite was in another direction, but he also mentioned that if we climbed this little slope, there was a spot with a much wider view.

By then, we had already noticed something.

Karuppan was very curious about that uphill trail.

He was already climbing it.

So we looked at each other, looked at Raayappan, and without really making a decision, we simply said, "Why not?"

And we followed him up.

That little climb gave us a beautiful view, but it also gave me another reason to understand why Karuppan seemed to own this forest as much as Raayappan did.

And somewhere during that climb, Raayappan came up with his second riddle.

“சோறு ஆறண்ணா, பழம் எட்டண்ணாதா, இதென்ன?”

A meal costs six annas, while fruit costs eight annas. What is this?

At first, we were completely confused.

How could a meal cost less than a fruit?

Once again, the answer didn't make much sense when translated directly into English. The riddle depended entirely on the Tamil words.

The word "ettina" sounds like "eight annas," but in Tamil it can also refer to something that is within reach. So when he says "palam ettina", he means that if the fruit is within your reach, you can simply pluck it and eat it.

The first part works in a similar way. "Aaranna" sounds like "six annas," but it also resembles a Tamil expression referring to something cooling down.

So the meaning of the riddle was essentially this:

If the fruit is within your reach, you can eat it. But if the rice is still hot, you have to wait until it cools down.

Once again, I managed to crack it.

Raayappan immediately clapped and said, "Adheythaan!" — "That's it!"

Eventually, we reached the top.

It wasn't some glamorous cliff with a humongous panoramic view. There was nothing dramatic about it. But it was peaceful. The view was wide, quiet, and beautiful in its own way.

And then Karuppan found his spot.

The edge of the cliff was made of rocks, and in one part, there was a small pond-like depression in the rock. It wasn't big enough for a human. If I had tried to step into it, the water would probably have only reached my calf.

But for Karuppan, it was perfect.

It was actually just a small hole in the rock that had collected water from the previous day's rain. As soon as we reached the place, Karuppan jumped straight into it and started enjoying the cold water.

After a while, he climbed out and stood right at the edge of the rock, looking over the vast landscape in front of him.

At that moment, he reminded me of Mufasa standing at the edge of Pride Rock.

Of course, I had to take a picture.

That became one of my favourite photographs from the trek.

We spent some time there, talking and enjoying the view. At some point, Raayappan disappeared somewhere and came back after about ten minutes. We then continued walking.

A little further along the trail, we reached another lower cliff-like area. It wasn't a rocky cliff like the previous one. It was more like an open, flat stretch overlooking the landscape. We decided to stop there for our second rest.

And that's when Raayappan started singing.

It was a pure village folk song, the kind of song people traditionally sing while working in the fields under the scorching sun. When you're doing heavy physical work for hours, singing becomes a way of making the work lighter, passing the time, and keeping the energy going.

“Vella veluthavila, vellarikka vitha vile

Vella veluthavila, vellarikka vitha vile

Vethalai iruntha konjom kudenma…”

He repeated the same lines again, changing the reference from betel leaves to betel nuts.

These songs were not just about the words. They had a rhythm of their own, and that rhythm usually followed the rhythm of the work being done in the fields. If someone was cutting crops, the song would follow the rhythm of the cutting. If they were shaking grains from the plants, the song would move with that rhythm. The work itself became the beat.

And somehow, we caught that rhythm too.

We all started clapping along with Raayappan as he sang. After a while, one of the men from the other group even got up and started dancing to it. I couldn't photograph that moment because of his privacy, but honestly, it didn't matter. Some moments are better experienced than photographed.

The song itself was simple. It was about the morning, selling cucumbers, and playfully asking a woman to spare him some betel leaves or betel nuts.

But what fascinated me was how naturally these things were woven into his world.

Betel leaves, betel nuts, the traditions around them, the riddles he had just told us—these weren't things he had learned from somewhere. They were simply part of the life he had grown up with. They had found their way into his conversations, his riddles, and even his songs.

We were amazed listening to him.

And while he continued talking and showing us around, I started photographing him.

Some photographs were of Raayappan interacting with us, Raayappan and Karuppan together.

By then, it was becoming difficult to tell whether we were following them through the forest or whether they were simply taking us through their world.

The return journey to the campsite was almost a blur. We were busy talking, joking, and continuing our conversations with the other group, and before we knew it, we were back at the campsite.

We had initially planned to take a short nap before leaving, but we quickly dropped that idea. The Milky Way wasn't going to happen, but that didn't mean we had to end the trip there. We still had a few places we could visit before heading back home.

So we said our goodbyes to Raayappan and Karuppan, got back into Garuda, and started driving—not towards Coimbatore, but in the opposite direction.

Our first stop was Bheeman Waterfalls.

We had to trek a little and climb down a series of steps to reach the waterfall. And true to its name, Bheeman Waterfalls was powerful. The current was strong, and we had a really good bath there.

The tiredness from the long drive, the trekking, the rough night in the tent, and everything else seemed to disappear with that bath.

But there was one more thing the waterfall gave us.

Hunger.

My uncle Kalai suggested that we just eat somewhere nearby and start our journey back to Coimbatore as soon as possible. But we had already travelled all the way to Jawadhu Hills, crossing Vaniyambadi. And Ambur was only about 30 to 35 kilometres away.

As a foodie, I simply couldn't let that opportunity go.

Missing Ambur biryani when we were that close was something I knew I wouldn't be able to forgive myself for.

My uncle protested, but we took the right turn at Vaniyambadi and headed towards Ambur.

We had a great plate of Ambur biryani and finally started our drive back home.

Somewhere near Krishnagiri, the sun was slowly dropping towards the horizon.

I was behind the wheel, and the setting sun was falling directly on my face, covering it in a warm golden glow.

And that's when I started thinking about Raayappan's riddles.

Suddenly, something clicked.

The first riddle had come when we were making that steep climb through the forest.

The second one had come when we were climbing another uphill stretch towards the cliff with the wider view.

Both times, Raayappan had started talking to us, making us think, making us laugh, and making us concentrate on something completely different.

And suddenly, I realised what he had actually been doing.

He was distracting us from the climb.

While we were busy trying to solve his riddles, he was quietly taking our minds away from the effort we were putting into the climb.

It was such a simple thing.

But the thought brought a smile to my face.

There I was, driving west towards home, the setting sun lighting up my face, smiling like an idiot behind the wheel.

We had gone to Jawadhu Hills looking for the Milky Way.

We never got to see it.

But somehow, on the way back home, two people we had never planned to meet—Raayappan and Karuppan—were the ones who stayed in our minds.
