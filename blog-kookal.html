Mountains that made and marooned – Kookal

By Monish • 10 Min Read

“Homo sapiens aka Human Beings can survive without electricity for two days.” It may sound absurd, but once you witness it, it begins to make sense. Kookal, a serene village tucked near Kodaikanal, India, is surrounded by mountains on all sides—raising not only its altitude but also its natural charm. Yet, nature has another face. The very mountains that sculpt its paradise also impose seclusion: no signal, no smooth roads, and no easy way in or out. Behind the beauty were hardships—power cuts, erratic weather, and roadblocks. And yet, life in Kookal moved on. That resilience? It earned my deepest admiration.

The journey from Coimbatore to Kookal began like a dream—a flower bed of comfort. Wide highways, well-maintained roads, food stalls popping up every few kilometres, fuel stations aplenty, and perfect mobile network coverage. Everything screamed convenience… until we hit the ghat section. Suddenly, the roadside stalls thinned down to a lonely one or two. The roads weren’t built for showing off anymore—they were purely for navigating. The cell phone network began playing its favourite hill game: hide and seek. The ghat road was a mighty stretch of 60 km leading up to Kodaikanal.

Kodaikanal, the ever-charming hill station, lived up to its touristy reputation. It had everything—plenty of eateries, shopping spots, and attractions. No wonder it’s one of South India’s most beloved hill destinations. Established by the British in the 1800s, they were smitten by the town’s chill weather that lasted all year round. The name ‘Kodaikanal’ even has two etymological tales, both linking back to the colonial era. One version splits it into two Tamil words—‘Kodai’ and ‘Kanal’, translating to ‘Summer-Sight’. The other interpretation? ‘Gift of the Forest’. And both make perfect sense. After all, the British did use it as a summer retreat, and the region is lush with dense forests.

After fueling up—both literally and figuratively—with food and supplies at Kodaikanal town (because Kookal wasn’t going to spoil us with options), we began the next leg. And that’s when the real journey kicked in. Kookal is a small, secluded village about 40 km away from Kodaikanal, and let’s just say... the roads were not what you'd call “road-trip friendly.” They were narrow, occasionally broken, and forced us to slow down each time a vehicle came from the opposite direction—which was often enough to test our patience.

We were driving a Citroën C3 Petrol variant. Though people casually label it a ‘hatchback’, the 180mm ground clearance it boasts definitely lives up to the manufacturer’s ‘compact SUV’ claim. That extra clearance was a blessing—allowing us to gracefully glide over potholes and broken patches. As for navigation, we’d come prepared. Offline maps? Check. Downloaded playlists? Check. Lessons learned from past mishaps? Double check. We refer to me—Monish, my brother Kiruthic, and my cousin Surjith. The final 10 km was an adventure in itself. A forest road so narrow that every oncoming vehicle required a complete stop-and-wait session. By now, Airtel had long since breathed its last, and Jio was gasping along with a bar or two every now and then. But all the struggles, all the bumps, the slowdowns, the sighs, and the occasional curses evaporated the moment we reached Kookal.

A pristine little village nestled beautifully between three towering mountain walls with an open valley stretching wide on the fourth side. It was pre-monsoon season in South India, and the rain had already started tapping its way into many regions. Kodaikanal had treated us to some gentle showers, making the landscape even more enchanting. But Kookal… well, Kookal was on a whole different wavelength of magic.

We stayed at a scenic little resort called Kookal Bliss, which truly lived up to its name. By day, our window opened up to an endless valley view; by night, we could spot faint city lights twinkling in the distance—like the world was saying hello from afar. The resort keeper, a warm and cheerful guy named Rocky, welcomed us with full gusto and guided us to our room—Room No. 6. I’ll remember that number for the rest of my life, because just a few steps from its door stood the edge of a drop so steep, one misstep and you'd be free-falling straight into the mountain valley below.

We stood there, frozen by the sight—not from fear, but awe. The wind was relentless, pushing us gently but firmly. Technically, it was just a breeze... but one that had sprinted unchallenged for kilometers, building momentum and muscle. It felt more like wind’s gym-trained cousin. There was a strange, sacred silence in that place. The kind you rarely find. It was broken only by the deep hum of the wind and the faint murmur of water flowing somewhere far below in the valley. I sat on the swing near our room, facing that spectacular view—completely unaware of the passing time, the soft drizzle that raised the chill a few notches, or the invisible sun quietly clocking out behind the clouds.

It was the darkness that nudged me back to reality. Now, while each room came with its own Wi-Fi (pretty impressive for a mountain village), here came the catch—thanks to the pre-monsoon rains, Kookal had been without power for the past 24 hours. A warm welcome, indeed. Thankfully, our phones had some grace charge left from the car ride, but there was no signal, no functioning Wi-Fi, and the resort’s backup power had already given up. The humble generator became our savior for charging phones. So, there we were—parading our way to get some battery juice and hunting for any food options from the nearest shop that still had life left in it.

As I sat guarding our charging phones and half-listening to Rocky bro’s conversations with his friends, Kiruthic and Surjith had set off under the umbrella provided by Rocky bro, hunting for food. Rocky bro, not a native of Kookal, visited only on weekends to maintain the resort. He was passionately sharing his experiences and wisdom with a friend interested in starting a resort of his own—spilling everything from operational insights to cost breakdowns and occasionally dropping subtle warnings: “Stay away from anything illegal, no matter how profitable it looks.” And there I was—just a tired guest getting unintentionally enlightened. About an hour later, Kiruthic and Surjith returned—not just with food, but with a story.

Kiruthic's account:

“We were walking like a romantic couple under the umbrella—except I was stuck with this guy. The darkness was suffocating. We stepped out like idiots without a flashlight, and the partial moonlight wasn’t helping at all. But with time, came comfort. Our eyes adapted, and the moon finally got to work. The dirt road was now a slushy mess—looked like over-oiled gravy and felt just as slippery. Each step needed precision. Darkness, mud, uncertainty... sounded more like a horror movie title than a food run.

Finally, we reached a stall where two men were struggling with their massive dosa tawa—rain kept interrupting their fire. The place had a waiting list already, so we moved on.

The next place was around 300 sq ft, lit only by a flickering battery lamp—yes, I counted, it flickered every 25 seconds. Inside were three people: a man, his wife, and their small daughter. Let’s call the man ‘Parotta Shop Anna’. And he was nothing short of a superhero. One second, he was flattening dough; the next, handing it to his wife, who tirelessly flipped parottas over a big wood stove. Then he scrambled eggs, packed orders, mixed masalas, fried fish, and even managed to smile while handing over our parottas.

His wife matched his energy—relentless, focused. And the little girl? She wandered between tables like she owned the place, eventually curling up on one to sleep. In between all this, Anna gently paused for a moment—just to cover his daughter with a piece of cloth—and went back to the grind like nothing happened. We came back not just with food, but with awe.”

With the little charge left on our phones, we braced ourselves for a night lit only by flashlights in that pitch-dark world. (Of course, no trip is complete without some quality late-night deep talks.) Kookal had just one dish to offer—parathas—and honestly, in the middle of the pre-monsoon chaos, we were grateful even for that. Then came the bombshell from Rocky: Kodaikanal and its surrounding regions were placed under a red alert by the meteorological department. That explained the increasingly aggressive rain since evening. He warned us that power might not return for another day due to a serious fault in the power line connecting Kodaikanal to Kookal. We had already paid for a two-night stay—or even if we hadn’t, we weren’t ready to leave this mystical world and return to reality just yet. Big mistake.

Unaware of how grave that mistake would turn out to be, we spent the night chilling, barely sleeping. The next morning, we took our own sweet time to wake up, and finally by noon, we set out for some sightseeing. The snacks we’d packed turned into our breakfast—because our lazy selves weren’t fit to move, let alone hunt for food. We roamed around a few viewpoints, relaxed by the beautiful Kookal Lake, and once again returned to our room with—you guessed it—another round of parathas.

Surprisingly, the village showed no signs of disruption. Life went on as if nothing had changed. Small shops remained open, cattle wandered freely, and people carried on with their chores as if a power cut wasn’t even worth noticing. Meanwhile, we were busy fighting over the car charger just to juice up our signal-less phones. Beyond serving as flashlights, music players, and cameras, they had no purpose in that world.

With that humbling realization, we walked to a tiny home-run shop to grab dinner early. The intensifying rain made it nearly impossible to walk even half a kilometer under an umbrella on those dark, slushy roads at night. And what did we get again? Parathas. Now, I love parathas—honestly, I do. But having the same kind, with the exact same gravy, for two straight days? That’s enough to bore any human soul.

Another beautiful moment came when we met a woman we came to call “home shop akka” who ran that tiny store from her home—maybe in her early thirties—with two children, a little girl and a boy we never fully saw. The boy was playing on a homemade swing inside, endlessly chattering with his mother and sister. From where we stood, we could only catch glimpses of his legs as the swing moved. He found joy in everything—the chirp of a bird, his sister’s giggles, the scent of egg fried rice being made in the kitchen. And then, the power came back on.

“Yaaaay!” he shouted, thrilled.

But seconds later, it vanished again. He frowned and said, “Next time I won’t cheer for the current. Maybe it leaves because I’m cheering.” True to his word, the next time the power returned, he stayed quiet—and the electricity stayed. Softly, he whispered to his mother: “I didn’t celebrate this time.” But fate played tricks again. Five minutes later, the power went out once more—and the little one scolded his mother: “You cheered, didn’t you?”

We laughed. We talked a little with akka, about the frequent power cuts and our experience in Kookal. She mentioned the unpredictability of life there and how people simply adapt. Before we could ask more, she rushed to pick up her toddler who had fallen. And there we stood again—amazed. She ran the shop, took care of two kids, kept her house running, and still found time to smile at strangers. If Parotta Anna was a superhero, then she was nothing short of Wonder Woman.

Later, in our room, whenever the power returned, we’d place our fingers on our lips—not to jinx it. That playful ritual, gifted to us by a boy we never fully saw, somehow lingered through the night.

The climate had no intention of being any kinder, so we prepared to endure yet another night with just flashlights. The power came by 8 PM but failed us again by 10 PM. The increasing chill in the air forced us to wrap ourselves in the resort’s provided blankets, and between random chit-chat and cozy warmth, we unknowingly drifted off to sleep.

As per the plan, we were up and ready by 4 AM the next morning and made our way down to the car park. The rain had finally stopped, but clear signs of a heavy downpour from the previous night were all around us. With the car’s music system blaring to chase away the lingering sleep, we drove into the narrow road flanked by dense forest. This was the same road that felt painfully tight during our arrival—but now, with no vehicles around at this early hour, it felt a tad easier. Yet that didn’t make the road any better. In fact, it was worse. The darkness was eerie, and only the stretch illuminated by our headlights was visible. The potholes and patchy stretches looked more menacing than ever.

The road was littered with leaves, twigs, and broken branches—scattered all over from the previous night’s storm. It made us question the wisdom of heading out this early. Still, our trusty compact SUV came to the rescue again. We rolled slowly over a few of the larger branches, and now, with the music turned off, silence filled the car—because the road demanded every bit of our focus.

And that’s when we fully realized the weight of the mistake we had made. An uprooted tree had completely blocked the road.

We hesitated to step out—not because of the drizzle, but because of the fear of animals. By then, we were deep in a known wildlife hotspot. At times of crisis, you fall back on your instincts. I stayed in the driver’s seat, hands on the steering wheel—ready to reverse, drive, or scare away any animal that might approach. Kiruthic and Surjith rolled up their sleeves, determined to clear the path. The car doors stayed open—just in case they had to leap back in within seconds.

Then we began the task. Fortunately, the tree wasn’t too large; just enough to be managed by two people with effort and grit. But as they pushed it to the side of the road, the trunk bounced back elastically, trying to reclaim its place. That’s where my driving instincts kicked in. I acted quickly, eased the car forward in a swift maneuver, and drove past the tree just in time. Kiruthic and Surjith jumped back in, grinning, thinking this was the final hurdle.

Little did they know—it was just the beginning.

A few more large branches blocked our path, though thankfully, they only needed one of us to step out and move them aside. Since we had no water to wash our hands after the messy task, we sacrificed one of our towels—turning it into an oversized tissue for the rest of the journey. By around 4:40 AM, we finally reached the slightly broader road that connects directly to Kodaikanal. That’s when we met our next big obstacle—another fallen tree.

But this time, it was serious. Even with all three of us pushing with full strength, the tree didn’t budge an inch. It was thicker, heavier, and clearly not something we could handle alone. We stood in silence, frustration sinking in, mentally juggling our schedules—we had work back in Coimbatore by afternoon. About five minutes passed—five long minutes of effort gone to waste—when we noticed a bright light piercing through the forest.

A vehicle was approaching.

The headlight beams scattered through the mist and branches, creating brilliant rays that danced with suspended raindrops. Metaphorically, it looked like a ray of hope breaking through—“Locals! Yes! They’ll definitely have a way to get rid of these tree-monsters,” we muttered to ourselves.

A rugged jeep approached, with three local men inside. They came forward cautiously—perhaps thinking we had stopped due to wild animals. But a quick exchange of pleasantries later, they got down to business. They had ropes ready. Without wasting time, they tied one end to the tree and the other to the bumper of their jeep. I moved our car aside to give them space, and the jeep began to reverse slowly.

Then came the sounds we didn’t want to hear: TUK... TUK... THUPPPP... SCREEECHHHH.

One by one, the ropes snapped. With each failure, our hope dimmed. Eventually, the jeep halted—ropeless and powerless. The locals suggested waiting until 6 AM. A goods truck usually passed through by then, and it might carry sturdier ropes. But none of us liked the idea of standing idle, so we examined the tree again and noticed a weak spot near the trunk. It gave us an idea.

Together—three of us and three of them—we coordinated a plan, adjusted our angle, and with combined effort, we yanked the trunk sideways. Miraculously, it cracked. The tree broke just enough to be rolled aside, clearing the path.

Mission accomplished—but not without consequences. Surjith now clutched his lower back, groaning in pain from a cramp. We pulled out our trusty towel tissue again for the cleanup and resumed the journey—this time trailing the jeep, relieved.

But fate? It looked back at us with a creepy little smile. Just as we glided ahead slowly, two more trees blocked our way—but they were small, and we pulled them aside with ease. And then it happened: we spotted a vehicle approaching from the opposite direction. That could only mean one thing—no more blockades ahead. Relieved, we decided to speed up, overtaking the localities’ jeep and bidding them a cheerful goodbye as we drove parallel for a moment.

But luck, as always, is a dramatic character. We soon encountered another fallen tree—this time, its trunk was wider than our car. Yet fate had spared us again. The tree had toppled slightly away from its roots, leaving just enough space for our vehicle to squeeze through. If that hadn’t happened, we’d have been stranded for a day or two, with no alternate road to escape to the outside world. That narrow escape was a big relief.

But it didn’t last long—just 15 minutes later, a big but movable tree lay across the road, completely blocking our path. This time, we didn’t even attempt anything at first. We just stood still, mentally replaying the roller coaster of events we’d just survived. Soon, the locals caught up with us—grinning, as if to say, “told you so.” One of them even teased us for rushing ahead without him. Still, that didn’t stop them from working with us.

Even with six of us pushing together, the tree didn’t budge. Right then, another car arrived from the opposite side, carrying five more men. With the grit and coordination of eleven determined minds and bodies, we tackled the obstacle. One branch at a time, we broke through. Finally, the massive trunk gave way—not to tools or machines, but to sheer force and testosterone.

But it seemed the locals had taken it a little personally. Once the path was slightly cleared for their vehicle, they moved ahead without helping us cross. Still, with eight people remaining, we managed to widen the path a little more and get through. We thanked the opposite party and made sure they passed safely before continuing on.

There were a few more fallen trees ahead, but thankfully, we weren’t the first to find them. Some had already been pushed aside, others broken just enough for a vehicle to pass through. By 6:30 AM, we finally reached Kodaikanal—delayed by a gracious 1 hour and 45 minutes. But honestly, we had no complaints. The hardships along the way felt like part of the experience, and every second was worth it.

The rest of the journey was uneventful. The roads were tourist-ready, without any further interruptions. This time, the car’s music system was off—not for focus, but for reflection. We were just visitors, passing through for a couple of days. But for the people of Kookal, this isn’t an adventure. This is their everyday life. And what felt like survival for us—fear, regret, second-guessing our choices—is just the routine for them. But no, I didn’t pity them. What I felt was something deeper: admiration. For Rocky bro, Parotta shop anna, home shop akka, that small boy—everyone. They live a life we struggle to adapt to for just a weekend, yet they never complain.

“You people come here for a day or two; think about our life. But we’re used to this—and we don’t complain. Maybe you should try our way too. Don’t complain. Just go with the flow.”

And somehow, that felt like advice not just for surviving Kookal, but for surviving life itself. Don’t complain. Just go with the flow.
